Western Classical Music Theory

A comprehensive guide to understanding classical music theory, harmonies, chord progressions, and exercises.

1. Introduction to Western Classical Harmony

Western Classical music has a rich history of harmony, where chords are used to create emotional and tonal structures. Harmony in classical music is typically based on functional harmony, where chords progress in predictable ways.

Major and Minor Scales

The foundation of Western classical harmony is based on the major and minor scales. These scales are used to build chords and create progressions.

Major Scale:

C Major Scale: C - D - E - F - G - A - B - C

Minor Scale:

A Minor Scale: A - B - C - D - E - F - G - A

Building Chords from Scales

Chords are built by stacking notes from the scale. In classical music, the most common chords are triads (three notes stacked in thirds).

Example 1: C Major Triad

The C Major chord is built from the notes of the C Major scale:

C Major: C - E - G

Example 2: A Minor Triad

The A Minor chord is built from the notes of the A Minor scale:

A Minor: A - C - E

2. Chord Progressions and Cadences

Chord progressions in classical music are sets of chords played in a specific order. Cadences are the ends of phrases or sections in music and help establish the key.

Common Chord Progressions

Here are some common chord progressions in classical music:

  • V - I (Perfect Cadence): This is the most common cadence in classical music, where the dominant (V) chord resolves to the tonic (I).
  • ii - V - I (Authentic Cadence): A common progression where the ii minor chord moves to the V dominant chord and resolves to the I major chord.
  • I - IV - V - I (Common Progression): This progression is often used in classical music and in various genres.

Example 1: I - IV - V - I in C Major

I - IV - V - I: C Major - F Major - G Major - C Major

3. Functional Harmony and Chord Functions

In classical music, harmony is often used to create tension and release. Chords are classified according to their function in a progression.

Chord Functions

  • Tonic (I): The tonic is the "home" chord, the most stable and resolved chord in the key.
  • Dominant (V): The dominant creates tension and leads back to the tonic.
  • Subdominant (IV): The subdominant is a preparatory chord that often leads to the dominant.

Example 1: Chord Functions in C Major

Tonic - Subdominant - Dominant - Tonic: C Major - F Major - G Major - C Major

4. Inversions and Voice Leading

Inversions involve rearranging the notes of a chord to create smoother voice leading between chords. Proper voice leading is a crucial skill in classical composition and arranging.

Chord Inversions

A chord inversion is when the lowest note of a chord is not the root note but one of the other notes in the chord.

Example 1: C Major Inversions

Root Position: C - E - G

First Inversion: E - G - C

Second Inversion: G - C - E

Voice Leading

Voice leading is the smooth transition from one chord to another, keeping individual voices close to each other in pitch. This is important for maintaining the harmonic flow of music.

5. Counterpoint

Counterpoint is the art of combining different melodic lines in a harmonious way. It is often used in classical compositions, particularly in the works of Bach.

Types of Counterpoint

  • Species Counterpoint: A system of counterpoint that includes different "species" or types, such as note against note, two notes against one, etc.
  • Fugal Counterpoint: A technique used in fugues, where the theme is introduced and imitated in different voices.

Exercise 1: Simple Counterpoint

Start by writing two melodic lines: one in the soprano and one in the alto. Ensure that they move in contrary motion to create harmony.

6. Classical Music Exercises

Here are some exercises to help you apply the theory of classical harmony to your playing and composition:

Exercise 1: Harmonic Analysis

Choose a classical piece, analyze its harmony, and identify the chord progressions and cadences used. Try to write out the chords and explain the functions of each chord.

Exercise 2: Compose a Short Piece

Using the I - IV - V - I progression, write a short melody in a major key and harmonize it with chords.

Exercise 3: Voice Leading Exercise

Practice writing chord progressions with proper voice leading. Aim to minimize the movement between voices and create smooth transitions between chords.
